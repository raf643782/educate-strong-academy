### Is Strongman Safe for Children? A Careful Guide for Parents, Coaches, and Clubs

If your child has shown interest in Strongman, or your school or club is considering introducing Strongman-style activities, it's reasonable to want a straight answer to a simple-sounding question. The honest answer isn't a simple yes or no, but there is good evidence to guide the decision, and this guide walks through what that evidence actually says.

#### The Short Answer

Strongman-style activity is not automatically safe for every child, and it is not automatically unsafe either. Recognised research on youth resistance training generally supports properly supervised, technique-focused, age-appropriate strength activity as a reasonable and potentially beneficial part of a child's physical development. But Strongman specifically involves awkward implements, loaded carries, and competition-style events that were designed for adults, and there is no dedicated body of research on applying these, unmodified, to children. The safest position is: general youth strength activity, done properly, is well supported; copying adult Strongman training directly is not something the evidence supports, and isn't something a responsible programme would do.

#### What Counts as Strongman for Children?

"Strongman for children" can mean quite different things depending on who's running it. At one end, it can mean scaled, technique-focused introductions to carrying, pressing, and lifting patterns, adapted for a child's size, strength, and coordination. At the other end, it could mean simply putting children through adult-style events with adult implements at reduced weight, which is a very different (and much less supported) proposition. This article focuses on the first approach, and treats the second as something to actively avoid.

#### What Youth Strength Training Research Actually Says

Youth resistance training itself, as distinct from Strongman specifically, has a substantial and reassuring evidence base. An international consensus statement on youth resistance training, endorsed by professional bodies across paediatric medicine, exercise science, and sports medicine, supports appropriately supervised resistance training as part of a young person's physical development. Recognised paediatric guidance distinguishes general movement based strength activity from more structured resistance training. It does not give a universal starting age for Strongman style activity, so parents and coaches should focus on the child's maturity, movement control, supervision, and the specific activity being offered. Guidance in this area consistently emphasises mastering technique with light resistance before load is increased, and increasing load gradually rather than in large jumps.

On one of the most persistent public concerns, a widely cited peer-reviewed review (Faigenbaum & Myer, British Journal of Sports Medicine, 2009) found that injury to growth cartilage ("growth plates") has not been reported in prospective youth resistance-training studies that provided professional guidance and instruction. The same review found that documented injuries linked to youth resistance training were generally attributed to inadequate supervision, poor technique, unsafe behaviour, or equipment issues, rather than to resistance training itself. In one dataset it cited, resistance training accounted for a notably small share of recorded youth sport injuries compared with American football. This is evidence about supervised, properly instructed training specifically, not a claim that any resistance activity is automatically risk-free.

#### How Strongman Style Training Is Different From Normal Youth Strength Training

This is where extra caution matters. The research above concerns general resistance training, typically using standard, well-understood equipment. Strongman implements, such as stones, logs, yokes, and sandbags, involve different technical and biomechanical demands. Research on adult Strongman movements has found, for example, that a log implement's greater diameter can change how a press loads the lower back compared with a standard barbell, and that certain adult Strongman movements carry particular mechanical demands worth understanding before coaching them. Separately, research on adult Strongman athletes has found a meaningful injury rate, with implement-specific training carrying a higher injury likelihood than traditional training in one study cohort.

None of that research was conducted on children, and there is no dedicated body of peer-reviewed research on Strongman-style training specifically in children. That gap is exactly why adult Strongman training and events should not simply be scaled down and handed to children. A responsible approach draws on the well-supported general youth resistance-training evidence, applies it with real caution around Strongman-specific implements, and does not assume adult competition formats are appropriate just because the weights have been reduced.

#### The Main Safety Factors Parents Should Look For

**Qualified and Suitable Supervision**

The research above is consistent on this point: qualified, attentive supervision is central to safe youth resistance training, and its absence is repeatedly linked to the injuries that do occur. Ask who is directly supervising the session and what their relevant background is.

**Age Appropriate Scaling**

Activities, implements, and loads should be genuinely adapted to the child's age, size, and stage of development, not simply a lighter version of an adult session using the same implements and format.

**Technique Before Load**

Recognised guidance consistently prioritises movement quality and technique over how much weight is being lifted, particularly early on. A session that rushes toward heavier loads before technique is consistent is a warning sign.

**No Maximal Lifting Culture**

Children should not be encouraged toward maximal or near-maximal single lifts. A well-run programme emphasises consistent, controlled technique over chasing a heaviest-ever number.

**Clear Safeguarding Practice**

Anyone working with children in a coaching or club setting should be operating within recognised safeguarding practice for their setting and country. In the UK, for example, the NSPCC's Child Protection in Sport Unit maintains a National Standards framework that sports organisations use to benchmark safe practice for children and young people. Parents are within their rights to ask what safeguarding arrangements a session or club has in place.

**Appropriate Equipment and Environment**

Implements, surfaces, and space should be suited to children, not simply adult equipment used at a lighter setting. Awkward or oversized implements designed for adults may not scale down safely just because less weight is loaded onto them.

#### What Coaches Need to Consider

**Movement Quality**

Coaching young athletes means prioritising consistent, correct movement patterns over load or event-specific performance, particularly with implements that behave differently from a standard barbell.

**Progression and Load Management**

General resistance-training progression principles, building capacity gradually and increasing demand in small steps, apply to children just as they do to adults, and arguably matter more given how much less research exists on child-specific Strongman implement use.

**Event Selection**

Not every adult Strongman event is an appropriate starting point for children. Coaches should think carefully about which movement patterns are genuinely suitable to introduce, rather than defaulting to whatever a local adult competition happens to use.

**Communication With Parents**

Parents should be able to understand what their child will actually be doing, how it will be supervised, and how progression will be managed, in plain terms rather than jargon.

**Scope of Practice**

Coaching children is not the same skill set as coaching adults, and a coach working with young athletes should be honest with themselves and with parents about where their own knowledge and experience genuinely extend to. For a broader look at what Strongman coaching involves generally, see our guide on becoming a Strongman coach.

#### What Schools, Clubs, and Organisations Should Consider

Organisations introducing Strongman-style activity for young people should think through supervision ratios, risk assessment, informed parental consent, suitability of the specific space and equipment, and how the activity fits within the organisation's existing safeguarding arrangements. None of this is unique to Strongman, but the awkward, less-standardised nature of Strongman implements makes it worth being particularly deliberate about these basics rather than assuming a general youth-sport risk assessment automatically covers it.

#### Common Mistakes to Avoid With Children

The clearest mistake is treating children as small adults, whether that means copying an adult training structure, adult event selection, or adult implements at a "lighter" setting. Other common issues include moving to heavier loads or more advanced implements before technique is consistent, and running sessions without supervision that is genuinely adequate for the group size and activity.

#### When Strongman Style Training May Not Be Appropriate

This article cannot give medical advice, and it shouldn't be used as a substitute for it. Recognised paediatric guidance identifies specific circumstances, such as uncontrolled high blood pressure, uncontrolled seizure disorders, certain cardiovascular conditions, or a history of chemotherapy, as reasons a child should be seen by a healthcare professional before starting a resistance-training programme. More generally, parents or carers should seek appropriate professional guidance where there are health concerns, injuries, medical conditions, or uncertainty, rather than relying on this article to make that judgement.

#### Frequently Asked Questions

**Is Strongman safe for children?**
Not automatically, and not automatically unsafe either. Properly supervised, age-appropriate, technique-focused strength activity has solid supporting evidence in general. Strongman-specific implements and adult competition formats need extra caution, since there's no dedicated research on applying these directly to children.

**What age can children start Strongman?**
There's no single, universal starting age for Strongman-style activity. Recognised paediatric guidance distinguishes general movement-based strength activity from more structured resistance training, and points to factors like a child's maturity, movement control, and the level of supervision available, rather than age alone. Strongman-specific activity should be introduced with particular caution, given the lack of dedicated research in this area.

**Should children lift heavy weights?**
Children should not be pushed toward maximal or near maximal lifts. Loads should be appropriate, controlled, technically sound, and progressed carefully under suitable supervision.

**Is youth strength training bad for growth plates?**
A widely cited peer-reviewed review found that growth-cartilage injury has not been reported in supervised youth resistance-training studies with proper instruction. Most injuries linked to youth resistance training were attributed to inadequate supervision or poor technique, not to resistance training itself.

**What should parents look for in a Strongman style session?**
Qualified supervision, genuine age-appropriate scaling (not just lighter adult equipment), a clear technique-before-load approach, no culture of maximal lifting, and clear safeguarding practice.

**Can schools or clubs run Strongman style activities?**
Potentially, yes, with proper risk assessment, appropriate supervision ratios, informed consent, suitable equipment, and alignment with the organisation's safeguarding arrangements.

**Is StrongKidz the same as adult Strongman training?**
No. It's intended as an age-appropriate, education-led approach rather than a scaled-down version of adult training. See the StrongKidz page for more.

**Should a child with an injury or medical condition do Strongman?**
Parents or carers should seek appropriate professional guidance where there are health concerns, injuries, medical conditions, or uncertainty, rather than assuming general guidance applies to their child's specific situation.
